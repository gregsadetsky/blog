I was giving away a ticket to a show of Cate Le Bon, an [incredible](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=pH6Lc4Ul9BQ) musician who has since become our "relationship godmother". We were together remotely through the pandemic, which I do not recommend. I can recognize FaceTime audio artifacts on a bad wifi connection by ear all too well now. Emily went to CMU to do a pretty well known masters in HCI, or human-computer interaction. You'd think that she is really good with computers and interfaces and humans, and she does happen to be very good at all of those. But if you were to ask me, before I knew, what I thought she did, I would just say "interfaces! the human ones, with computers" aka, I didn't have a clue.

It took me a minute, but now I know. Emily has worked for many years as a UX researcher - and these days, she's running a [research & design consultancy](https://saltern.studio/).

"So wait, UX... Like if a button feels good? You do research like you want to invent a new kind of button that's better? And probably A/B test it...?"

That's not what Emily did, and that's not what people in UX research mostly do. That's not what's cool about them or why they are so awesome.

I've grown weirdly angry the more I learned -- as a software developer, it seemed that not only did I not know about this field and their methods, but I also realized that I did not do in my work what they do. I was not only in the dark (which is fine - you can't know everything), but I also realized that what UX researchers care about is so extremely useful and important for software and product development that ... you'd think I would have seen it, or known about it after a year or twenty in the tech industry. And you'd also think that I would embrace it and do more of it, like, "naturally".

Well, I'm trying to be a better husband-of-UX-specialist. And I believe that AI will also help (I've also been accused of being a techno-optimist). But first things first.

## What is UX research

Okay, so me telling you what UX research is, is like, a really bad idea. I am not a specialist (did you read any of the above), and my version of it is as good as "some guy told me about it a party once but I only heard every 3rd word because the music was so loud". Emily's explained it to me many times. I'm just a slow learner and too humble to tell you that I can give you any useful or truthful or good definition. Yeah, no, I'm actually really super humble.

You could read up about it (Erika Hall's [*Just Enough Research*](https://www.mulebooks.com/just-enough-research) is a good short place to start). But ok, I'll tell you what I think UX research is.

UX researchers want to know how and why people interact with digital stuff (and even physical spaces... but that's for another time).

Why, not just how - why. What drives a person to a computer/phone.. - what problem, life situation or goal, truly, why life brought them to seek out something digital.

Where were they in their existence at that point, what led them to want something, and then - what do they think will solve their problems (what do they reach for) and how they go about doing it (my mom posting a picture on Facebook, my dad ordering on Amazon, me using Google Maps - different who's, and why's - and when's/how's, etc.)

The thing that stuck out and "hurt" the most to realize, was that this seeking of why felt "backwards" to me as someone who loves to "build first, and ask questions later". Now, I am convinced that this is clearly the better way of doing/building things. (I am not wife-pilled, ok).

Emily taught a class once about these methods of asking questions, listening and iterating, and I remain amused but also flabbergasted at one of the homeworks that the class had early on. Students were peer testing in small groups an app/product they were designing: for example, a banking app - and the students built small [paper prototypes](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/paper-prototyping/) that even had a small "sliding window" also made of paper. You could swipe options with your finger, and you could pretend to see more options by moving paper around. It felt like a lost art form of paper computers, maybe the kind that people used before electricity. I imagined presenting one to a king and calling it "amusing useful paper automata that one day will actually work." (for reference, Carolyn Snyder wrote [the book on it](https://www.oreilly.com/library/view/paper-prototyping/9781558608702/).)

I digress. But I don't digress. I've been "angry" ever since at having no natural tendency to ask why first, and to listen, and to work with people first (I do really love people, I promise). I have never made a paper software prototype! Do I need to state the obvious and say that it takes 3 minutes to make a software paper prototype?? and that it's really easy to change?

I just "did". I built stuff, sometimes a lot, sometimes a ton of it, on my own, for the pleasure of building stuff (building is fun, and that's ok of course!), while in the back of my mind thinking some variation of "they're going to love this!"

WHO IS "THEY"!!!

I never really asked.

(Turns out there's a name for where I was. Nielsen Norman Group (the UX people's UX people) has a [UX maturity model](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/ux-maturity-model/) with six stages, going from "Absent" (UX is ignored, or nobody sees a need for it) all the way to "User-driven" (user research steers what gets built -- very few organizations ever get there). Interestinly, their original model had a separate, *higher* stage for "developer-centered" UX - basically developers who care a lot and do it themselves. They've since folded it back down into stage 1, because the best intentions of hard-working developers aren't enough without actual research. That's me. I'm stage 1 with extra steps. There's a [short quiz](https://forms.nngroup.com/s3/Maturity-Quiz) if you want to find out where your team is.)

---

In this age of AI-written software, what's changed is that prototypes are free to make now. Paper and code are "the same". It takes less energy to write "no, differently - give me 4 different options" than using scissors. That's funny, and great. Progress.[^1]

[^1]: Reading this, Em chimed in that "there is something lost in this I think or worth mentioning about the value of low fidelity things" -- err, there's actually a lot more to say here probably... the TLDR is - here, as everywhere else in life - that asking AI to "just do the thing" (e.g. "make 4 prototypes") will not _necessarily_ solve _all_ of your problems for you. (sorry)

What has not changed is the non-tendency (mine! but maybe yours too?) to seek out users and to talk to them. And you know what - it's not easy at all. It's not easy to "recruit" (what UX'ers call finding people), to come up with the right open-ended questions, to interview, to [not talk](https://alistapart.com/article/interviewing-humans), and to integrate conflicting viewpoints. Humans are terribly/beautifully messy and you will face that messiness way more head-on than if you don't talk to users. (Small consolation: you don't need a stadium of users. NN/g's famous rule of thumb is that [five users](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/why-you-only-need-to-test-with-5-users/) will show you most of what's broken.[^2])

[^2]: But also Em says that it's good to be suspicious of magic numbers. Still, she adds, "I do agree with basic premise that you can learn a lot more than you think by just talking to a few people."

Well, that's it. I've rambled and complimented Emily and made a half point about AI. But, truly, I do think that something about user/software development ... antics? folklore? (ie talk to users to get "their input" once the software is done, not actually set up an interview plan that shapes what even is the software) would benefit from changing. More UX I say. More listening to your wives.

I love you kitten.
